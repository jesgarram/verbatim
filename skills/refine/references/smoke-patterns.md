# Smoke Patterns

Prose smoke is language that adds words without adding meaning. Unlike AI slop (which sounds like a machine wrote it), smoke often sounds fine on first read — it just makes the writing softer, slower, and less direct than it could be.

The Critic uses this list to detect smoke. The Editor decides what to cut, what to tighten, and what to keep because it serves the author's voice or rhythm.

## Tier 1: High-Confidence Cuts

Patterns that are nearly always deletable without losing meaning. Remove first, re-add only when genuinely intentional.

### Qualifier padding

Words that soften without adding meaning.

- "quite," "rather," "somewhat," "a bit," "a little," "sort of," "kind of" (when hedging, not when describing degree)
- "very," "really," "just," "pretty" (as intensifiers — "pretty good," "really interesting," "just think")
- "actually," "basically," "essentially," "literally" (when not literal), "honestly," "frankly"
- "in a sense," "to some extent," "to a degree," "in some ways"

### Filler constructions

Phrases that can be shortened or deleted.

- "in order to" → "to"
- "due to the fact that" / "for the reason that" / "on account of" → "because"
- "at this point in time" / "at the present time" → "now"
- "in the process of" → delete or use verb directly
- "the fact that" → usually cuttable
- "what I mean is" / "what I'm saying is" → just say it
- "needless to say" → then don't say it
- "it goes without saying" → same

### Redundant pairings

Where one word does the job of two.

- "each and every" → "every"
- "first and foremost" → "first"
- "any and all" → "all"
- "basic fundamentals" → "fundamentals"
- "future plans" → "plans"
- "past experience" → "experience"
- "completely finished" → "finished"
- "absolutely essential" → "essential"

## Tier 2: Selective Cuts

Patterns that require context. Often cuttable, but sometimes they carry voice or rhythm.

### Filter words

Words that create distance between the reader and the content.

- "I think," "I believe," "I feel" — often removable when the whole piece is clearly the author's opinion
- "I realized," "I noticed," "I found" — cut when the realization IS the content ("I realized it was broken" → "It was broken")
- "I tend to think," "my sense is," "my take is" — hedging posture; remove when the author is clearly stating their position

Note: Some filter words are the author's voice (thinking out loud, sharing the discovery process). The Editor decides.

### Passive constructions

Active voice is usually stronger. Flag passive for review.

- "It was decided that" → "We decided"
- "The system was built to" → "We built the system to"
- "Mistakes were made" → "We made mistakes"

Exception: passive is appropriate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when the object should carry the emphasis.

### Weak verb + adverb

A strong verb beats a weak verb propped up by an adverb.

- "walked slowly" → "ambled," "sauntered"
- "said loudly" → "shouted"
- "looked carefully" → "examined," "studied"

Important: preserve the author's actual vocabulary. Don't upgrade "ran fast" to "sprinted" if the author would say "ran fast." The voice profile governs this.

### State-of-being openers

"There is/are" and "It is" constructions often hide the real subject.

- "There are many reasons why" → "Many reasons explain why" or just state the reasons
- "It is important to note that" → cut entirely or state the thing
- "There was a problem with" → name the problem directly

## Tier 3: Structural Cuts

Highest impact, highest risk. These catch waste above the sentence level.

### Sentence-level redundancy

The same idea restated in different words within the same paragraph.

Example: "The system was slow. Performance was terrible. Everything took forever." Three sentences, one idea. Keep the most vivid; cut the rest.

Detection: Look for adjacent sentences where removing one doesn't lose information.

### Section-level redundancy

A section that restates what a previous section already established, just with different framing.

Detection: Can you summarize two sections identically? If yes, one is redundant.

### Tangential asides

Parenthetical commentary or examples that interrupt the flow without serving the argument.

Detection: Remove the aside. Does the paragraph still work? Is anything lost?

Note: Some tangential asides ARE the author's voice — humor, personality, warmth. The Editor decides.

## How the Critic Uses This

Flag specific instances with exact quotes. Reference the pattern category and tier. Prioritize based on density: a few qualifiers are natural rhythm. A paragraph loaded with them is smoke — the author backing away from their own ideas.

## How the Editor Uses This

Not every flagged pattern needs cutting. The question is always: does this word earn its place? If it carries voice, rhythm, or deliberate tone — keep it. If it's just there — cut it.

Think of Perkins cutting Wolfe: remove the scaffolding, keep the building. The goal is prose that's lean, not stripped.
