# Refinement Principles

These are the criteria the Critic evaluates against. They focus on reader experience and content quality. Voice and style are out of scope — those belong to the author.

## 1. The Opening Earns Attention

The first two paragraphs give the reader a reason to keep going. This could be a surprising claim, a concrete problem they recognize, or a story that pulls them in. Generic setup ("In today's world...") or a buried hook means the opening isn't working.

## 2. Every Section Earns Its Length

Each section advances the piece. If a section repeats a point already made, wanders without arriving anywhere, or could be cut without the reader noticing, it's not earning its length. Short and sharp beats long and thorough.

## 3. Claims Are Backed by Specifics

When the author makes a claim ("this approach is better", "we saw improvements"), there should be a concrete example, a number, a story, or an analogy nearby. Unbacked assertions feel hand-wavy. The reader should be able to picture what the author is describing.

## 4. Transitions Carry the Reader

Moving from one section to the next feels natural. If the reader has to mentally bridge a gap ("wait, how did we get here?"), the transition needs smoothing. But sometimes a clean break is the right transition — over-connecting is just as bad.

## 5. The Piece Lands Somewhere

By the end, the reader can answer: "what's the one thing this person wants me to know?" The ending doesn't need to be a formal conclusion, but it needs to arrive. Trailing off into vague "food for thought" or just stopping mid-arc is a problem.

## 6. No Filler, No Throat-Clearing

Sentences that exist to warm up, hedge excessively, or repeat what's already implied are filler. "It's worth noting that" adds nothing. "Basically" before an explanation that's no simpler than the original adds nothing. Flag specific instances with the exact words.

## 7. Technical Concepts Are Accessible

If the author introduces a technical concept, the reader understands it from context — through analogy, example, or explanation. A concept dropped in without support is a speed bump. But domain terms that the target audience would know are fine.

## 8. The Pacing Keeps Moving

Read the piece for rhythm. Any section that feels like it's going on too long, any energy dip in the middle, any stretches of similar-length paragraphs creating monotony — those are pacing issues. They are felt more than seen.
